Part 1: Multiple-Choice Questions (20)

Choose ONE correct answer from the four options.

Question 1. What is an open economy?

  • A. An economy that does not trade with other countries.
  • B. An economy that interacts with other countries through trade and financial flows.
  • C. An economy that produces only agricultural products.
  • D. An economy that prohibits foreign investment.
Correct answer: B. An economy that interacts with other countries through trade and financial flows.
Explanation: An open economy engages in international trade, imports, exports, foreign investment, and cross-border financial transactions.

Question 2. How do we calculate net exports (NX)?

  • A. Imports minus exports.
  • B. Consumption minus investment.
  • C. Exports minus imports.
  • D. Government spending minus taxes.
Correct answer: C. Exports minus imports.
Explanation: The formula is NX = X - M. If exports are $500 million and imports are $400 million, net exports are $100 million, a trade surplus.

Question 3. Which formula represents national income (GDP) in an open economy?

  • A. Y = C + I + G
  • B. Y = C + I + G + (X - M)
  • C. Y = C - I - G
  • D. Y = X + M - G
Correct answer: B. Y = C + I + G + (X - M)
Explanation: Open-economy GDP includes consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports. Imports are subtracted to remove foreign-produced goods and services.

Question 4. If 1 USD = 4,100 KHR, how many riels can you exchange for $100?

  • A. 4,100 KHR
  • B. 40,100 KHR
  • C. 400,000 KHR
  • D. 410,000 KHR
Correct answer: D. 410,000 KHR.
Explanation: 100 × 4,100 = 410,000 KHR.

Question 5. From Cambodia's perspective, what type of quotation is 1 USD = 4,100 KHR?

  • A. Direct quotation.
  • B. Indirect quotation.
  • C. Real exchange rate.
  • D. Purchasing power parity.
Correct answer: A. Direct quotation.
Explanation: A direct quotation expresses the price of one unit of foreign currency in domestic currency.

Question 6. The exchange rate falls from 4,100 KHR/USD to 4,000 KHR/USD. What happens to the riel against the dollar?

  • A. It depreciates.
  • B. It becomes worthless.
  • C. It appreciates.
  • D. It remains unchanged.
Correct answer: C. It appreciates.
Explanation: One US dollar now buys fewer riels, so the riel has strengthened against the dollar.

Question 7. What is the real exchange rate?

  • A. The rate determined only by the central bank.
  • B. The nominal exchange rate adjusted for relative price levels.
  • C. The rate used only by commercial banks.
  • D. The amount of money printed by a country.
Correct answer: B. The nominal exchange rate adjusted for relative price levels.
Explanation: The real exchange rate considers the nominal exchange rate and price levels, helping compare relative prices and competitiveness.

Question 8. What does the Nominal Effective Exchange Rate (NEER) measure?

  • A. A weighted average of nominal exchange rates against trading partners.
  • B. The price of one product in two countries.
  • C. Domestic inflation only.
  • D. Foreign currency held by commercial banks.
Correct answer: A. A weighted average of nominal exchange rates against trading partners.
Explanation: NEER summarizes movements against a basket of currencies, generally weighted by trade importance. REER also adjusts for relative prices or costs.

Question 9. If foreign consumers increase demand for Cambodian goods, what is likely to happen to demand for the riel?

  • A. Demand for KHR falls to zero.
  • B. Supply of KHR must disappear.
  • C. Demand for KHR shifts left.
  • D. Demand for KHR shifts right, putting upward pressure on its value.
Correct answer: D. Demand for KHR shifts right, putting upward pressure on its value.
Explanation: Foreign buyers may need riels to purchase Cambodian goods. Greater demand can cause appreciation, other things equal.

Question 10. What is a likely effect of domestic currency depreciation on trade?

  • A. Exports automatically become more expensive for foreign buyers.
  • B. Exports become cheaper for foreign buyers and imports more expensive for domestic buyers.
  • C. Exports and imports always disappear.
  • D. The trade balance must improve immediately.
Correct answer: B. Exports become cheaper for foreign buyers and imports more expensive for domestic buyers.
Explanation: Depreciation can improve competitiveness, but the trade balance may not improve immediately because quantities adjust over time.

Question 11. What is the main idea of the Law of One Price?

  • A. Every country must use the same currency.
  • B. All products must have the same production cost.
  • C. Identical tradable goods should have the same price in a common currency under ideal conditions.
  • D. Interest rates must be equal everywhere.
Correct answer: C. Identical tradable goods should have the same price in a common currency under ideal conditions.
Explanation: The idea assumes comparable goods, competitive markets, and no significant transportation costs or trade barriers.

Question 12. Under Relative PPP, what tends to happen when domestic inflation is higher than foreign inflation? Assume E is domestic currency per unit of foreign currency.

  • A. The domestic currency tends to depreciate over time.
  • B. It must appreciate immediately.
  • C. The exchange rate can never change.
  • D. Inflation has no relationship with exchange rates.
Correct answer: A. The domestic currency tends to depreciate over time.
Explanation: Approximately, %ΔE = domestic inflation - foreign inflation. Higher domestic inflation tends to make E rise.

Question 13. Which belongs to the current account of the balance of payments?

  • A. Only purchases of foreign shares.
  • B. Only central bank reserves.
  • C. Only foreign direct investment.
  • D. Trade in goods and services, income, and current transfers.
Correct answer: D. Trade in goods and services, income, and current transfers.
Explanation: The current account includes trade, income, and current transfers such as qualifying remittances.

Question 14. What is mainly recorded in the financial account?

  • A. Domestic consumer spending.
  • B. Cross-border financial investment and transactions in financial assets.
  • C. Government tax revenue only.
  • D. Prices of exported goods.
Correct answer: B. Cross-border financial investment and transactions in financial assets.
Explanation: Examples include foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, and other financial flows.

Question 15. What is the main idea of Interest Rate Parity (IRP)?

  • A. Every country must have the same interest rate.
  • B. Exchange rates never change when interest rates change.
  • C. Interest-rate differences are linked to exchange-rate relationships, limiting risk-free arbitrage.
  • D. Foreign investments always earn higher returns.
Correct answer: C. Interest-rate differences are linked to exchange-rate relationships, limiting risk-free arbitrage.
Explanation: Covered IRP links domestic and foreign interest rates with spot and forward exchange rates, subject to market conditions.

Question 16. What distinguishes Covered IRP from Uncovered IRP?

  • A. Covered IRP uses a forward contract to hedge exchange-rate risk.
  • B. Covered IRP ignores interest rates.
  • C. It applies only without foreign investment.
  • D. It requires fixed exchange rates.
Correct answer: A. Covered IRP uses a forward contract to hedge exchange-rate risk.
Explanation: A forward contract sets a rate for a future transaction; uncovered IRP uses an expected future spot rate instead.

Question 17. Under Uncovered IRP, which exchange rate is used to assess expected future returns?

  • A. A guaranteed future rate.
  • B. A permanently fixed rate.
  • C. A randomly selected rate.
  • D. The expected future spot exchange rate.
Correct answer: D. The expected future spot exchange rate.
Explanation: Because the future spot rate is uncertain, the realized foreign investment return may differ from the expected return.

Question 18. What does the J-curve effect explain?

  • A. Exports must always equal imports.
  • B. Depreciation may initially worsen the trade balance before improving it later.
  • C. Central banks cannot change interest rates.
  • D. Inflation is always zero under fixed exchange rates.
Correct answer: B. Depreciation may initially worsen the trade balance before improving it later.
Explanation: Contracts and slow quantity adjustments can cause the import bill to rise before trade quantities respond.

Question 19. Under a floating exchange rate, what may follow expansionary monetary policy, other things equal?

  • A. The exchange rate becomes permanently fixed.
  • B. Capital flows and exchange rates no longer matter.
  • C. Lower interest rates may encourage outflows and depreciation, potentially supporting net exports.
  • D. Imports automatically fall to zero.
Correct answer: C. Lower interest rates may encourage outflows and depreciation, potentially supporting net exports.
Explanation: The effect depends on economic conditions, but interest rates can influence capital flows and exchange rates.

Question 20. What is the Impossible Trinity?

  • A. Choosing between exports, imports, and consumption.
  • B. Inability to achieve full employment, zero inflation, and zero taxes simultaneously.
  • C. Inability to maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and independent monetary policy simultaneously.
  • D. A requirement that every country have three currencies.
Correct answer: C. Inability to maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and independent monetary policy simultaneously.
Explanation: A country must give up at least one of these three policy goals.

Part 3: Questions & Answers (4)

Use the model answers to practice explaining concepts in your own words.

Question 1. Explain the differences between the nominal exchange rate, real exchange rate, NEER, and REER.

Answer:

The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in another, such as 1 USD = 4,100 KHR. The real exchange rate adjusts the nominal rate for relative price levels and helps compare relative prices and competitiveness. The Nominal Effective Exchange Rate (NEER) is a weighted average of exchange rates against major trading partners. The Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) further adjusts an effective rate for relative prices or costs.

Brief explanation: The nominal rate is a quoted currency value; the real rate also considers prices. Effective measures cover a basket of trading-partner currencies rather than only one.

Question 2. Explain PPP and the difference between Absolute PPP and Relative PPP.

Answer:

Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) is the theory that exchange rates are related to the purchasing power of currencies. Absolute PPP suggests comparable baskets should have the same price in a common currency under ideal assumptions. If E is domestic currency per unit of foreign currency, E_PPP = P_d / P_f. Relative PPP focuses on changes over time: %ΔE = π_d - π_f. For example, if domestic inflation is 6% and foreign inflation is 2%, E is expected to rise by about 4%, implying domestic-currency depreciation under this quotation convention.

Brief explanation: Absolute PPP compares price levels at a point in time; Relative PPP relates inflation differences to exchange-rate changes. Actual rates can deviate, especially in the short run.

Question 3. What is the Balance of Payments? Explain its three main accounts as presented in the chapter.

Answer:

The Balance of Payments (BOP) systematically records a country's economic transactions with the rest of the world over a period. The current account records goods, services, income, and current transfers. The capital account records capital transfers and transactions involving certain non-produced, non-financial assets. The financial account records cross-border financial investment and financial asset transactions, including direct investment, portfolio investment, and other financial flows.

Brief explanation: The accounts classify different types of international transactions. Trade and investment transactions can create demand for, or supply of, foreign currencies.

Question 4. Explain the main exchange-rate regimes and the Impossible Trinity.

Answer:

A fixed exchange rate is maintained at a target value or within a narrow range by the monetary authority. A floating exchange rate is primarily determined by market supply and demand. A managed float is mainly market-driven, but the monetary authority may intervene. The Impossible Trinity says a country cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and independent monetary policy; it must give up at least one goal.

Brief explanation: Exchange-rate regimes determine how much flexibility the currency has and how much control the monetary authority can exercise over monetary conditions.

Part 4: Case Studies (4)

Show calculation steps for numerical questions.

Case Study 1: Currency Conversion, Appreciation, and Depreciation

Scenario: A Cambodian importer must pay $2,000. The rate changes from 1 USD = 4,100 KHR to 1 USD = 4,300 KHR.
  1. Calculate the original cost in riels.
  2. Calculate the new cost and the increase.
  3. Did the riel appreciate or depreciate? What are likely trade effects?
Answers and Explanations:
  1. $2,000 × 4,100 = 8,200,000 KHR. (Explanation: Multiply the USD payment by the KHR-per-USD rate).
  2. $2,000 × 4,300 = 8,600,000 KHR. Increase = 8,600,000 - 8,200,000 = 400,000 KHR. (Explanation: The importer needs 400,000 more riels to make the same USD payment).
  3. The riel depreciated. Dollar-priced imports become more expensive for Cambodian buyers, while Cambodian goods may become cheaper for foreign buyers. Net exports may improve over time if quantities respond sufficiently. (Explanation: Depreciation changes relative prices, but trade quantities and contracts may adjust slowly).

Case Study 2: Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

Scenario: A basket costs 400,000 KHR in Cambodia and $100 in the US. The market rate is 1 USD = 4,100 KHR.
  1. Calculate the PPP exchange rate.
  2. Compare the market rate with PPP.
  3. Why can the market rate differ from PPP?
Answers and Explanations:
  1. E_PPP = 400,000 KHR / $100 = 4,000 KHR/USD. (Explanation: Divide the domestic basket price by the foreign basket price under this quotation convention).
  2. Difference = 4,100 - 4,000 = 100 KHR/USD. Percentage difference relative to PPP = (100 ÷ 4,000) × 100 = 2.5%. (Explanation: The market rate is 2.5% higher than the PPP benchmark; under this simplified comparison, the riel is weaker relative to the dollar than the PPP benchmark suggests).
  3. Reasons include transport costs, tariffs, trade barriers, product-quality differences, non-tradable goods and services, capital flows, interest rates, expectations, and speculation. (Explanation: PPP is a benchmark, not a guarantee that the market rate equals the PPP rate at every moment).

Case Study 3: Interest Rate Parity and Foreign Investment

Scenario: An investor has $10,000. US interest is 5%; euro interest is 3%. Spot rate: 1 USD = 0.90 EUR. One-year forward rate: 1 USD = 0.883 EUR. Ignore taxes and transaction costs.
  1. Calculate the domestic investment's final value.
  2. Calculate the foreign investment's final value in euros.
  3. Convert the foreign investment back to dollars using the forward rate.
  4. Why is a forward contract important?
Answers and Explanations:
  1. $10,000 × (1 + 0.05) = $10,500. (Explanation: The investor earns $500 interest over one year).
  2. Initial euros = $10,000 × 0.90 = €9,000. Final value = €9,000 × 1.03 = €9,270. (Explanation: The euro investment earns €270 interest over one year).
  3. €9,270 ÷ 0.883 = $10,498.30. This is about $1.70 less than the $10,500 domestic return. (Explanation: The illustrative rates are rounded. In a frictionless market, Covered IRP links the rates so comparable covered returns are equal).
  4. It fixes the future conversion rate for the euro proceeds, reducing exchange-rate uncertainty. (Explanation: Without the forward contract, the dollar value would depend on the future spot rate).

Case Study 4: Monetary Policy, Capital Flows, and the J-Curve

Scenario: Country A has a floating exchange rate. Its central bank cuts the interest rate from 5% to 3%, while the partner's rate remains 4%. The domestic currency depreciates, but trade quantities adjust slowly.
  1. How can the rate cut affect capital flows and the exchange rate?
  2. What effects can depreciation have on exports and imports?
  3. Why might the trade balance initially deteriorate?
  4. How does this illustrate the relationship between monetary policy, exchange rates, and output?
Answers and Explanations:
  1. Lower relative returns may make domestic assets less attractive and encourage capital outflows, putting downward pressure on the currency. (Explanation: Under a float, market supply and demand determine the exchange rate, and capital flows can affect both).
  2. Domestic goods may become cheaper for foreign buyers and foreign goods more expensive for domestic buyers. Exports may rise and imports may fall over time, potentially improving net exports. (Explanation: The final effect depends on quantity responses, production capacity, and other economic conditions).
  3. Existing import contracts and export orders may not change immediately. The import bill can rise before quantities adjust. (Explanation: This is the J-curve effect: the trade balance may worsen first and improve later).
  4. A rate cut may encourage capital outflows, leading to depreciation. Depreciation can support net exports over time, and changes in investment and net exports can affect aggregate demand and output. (Explanation: In an open economy, monetary policy operates through interest rates, capital flows, exchange rates, and net exports).

Final Revision Sheet - Key Formulas

  • 1. Net Exports: Exports minus imports. (NX = X - M)
  • 2. Open-Economy GDP: Y = C + I + G + (X - M) (Consumption + investment + government spending + net exports)
  • 3. Real Exchange Rate: q = e × (P* / P) (Use the exchange-rate and price-level convention specified in the question or slides)
  • 4. Absolute PPP: E_PPP = P_d / P_f (For domestic currency per unit of foreign currency, divide the domestic basket price by the foreign basket price)
  • 5. Relative PPP: %ΔE ≈ π_d - π_f (For domestic currency per unit of foreign currency, higher domestic inflation tends to make E rise over time)
  • 6. Covered IRP: 1 + i_d = (F / S)(1 + i_f) (This form assumes F and S are domestic currency per unit of foreign currency)
  • 7. One-year Future Value: Future Value = P(1 + i) (P is the initial principal and i is the annual interest rate in decimal form)

Five Concepts to Remember

  • 1. Appreciation vs. depreciation: For a quote in KHR per USD, a rise means the riel depreciates.
  • 2. PPP vs. IRP: PPP connects exchange rates with price levels and inflation; IRP connects interest rates with spot and forward rates.
  • 3. Balance of payments: Know the current, capital, and financial accounts.
  • 4. J-curve: Depreciation may worsen the trade balance initially before improving it later.
  • 5. Impossible Trinity: A country cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and independent monetary policy.
